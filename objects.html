Y-type brown dwarf · Hydra · ~7.4 light-years
WISE 0855-0714
The coldest known brown dwarf, about 7.4 light-years away. Its temperature is estimated at roughly −50 to 10 °C depending on the model, so it glows almost entirely in infrared and is nearly invisible to ordinary telescopes. Found in WISE survey data and announced in 2014, it holds only about 3–10 Jupiter masses and may have water-ice clouds.
Red dwarf · Ophiuchus · ~6 light-years
Barnard's Star
A small red dwarf only about 6 light-years away, the second-closest star system to the Sun. It holds the record for proper motion, drifting across the sky by 10.3 arcseconds every year. In 2025 astronomers confirmed four tiny, close-in planets around it, each with less than half the mass of Earth.
Binary brown dwarfs · Indus · ~12 light-years
Epsilon Indi B (Binary Dwarf Field)
Two brown dwarfs, Ba and Bb, circle each other about every 11 years and are the nearest known brown dwarfs, roughly 12 light-years away. Weighing about 67 and 53 Jupiter masses, both are cool T dwarfs, glowing at roughly 1,100 °C and 700 °C. Together they orbit the orange star Epsilon Indi A, more than 1,400 AU away.
Young T Tauri binary · Auriga · ~450–600 light-years
RW Aurigae (Dust Disk Variable Field)
A young pair of T Tauri stars still wrapped in dusty disks. Since 2010 the main star has faded unpredictably several times, by several magnitudes, and astronomers think clumps of dust drifting in front of it are responsible. Gas and dust torn out of its disk during a close pass by its companion may feed those clouds.
Mira variable · Draco · ~2,000 light-years
R Draconis (Pulsating Giant Field)
A pulsating red giant of the Mira type, swelling and shrinking over a cycle of about 246 days. In visible light it swings from magnitude 6.7 (binocular range) to 13.2, roughly 400 times fainter. Much of that swing is energy shifting between visible and infrared light as the star’s temperature changes, which is why infrared views matter.
